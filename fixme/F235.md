# F235. **[resolved]** the stop fade was a master fader that never re-opened, so a key pressed more than 1.5 s after the transport stopped is rendered and then multiplied by zero

**Henri's own reading, 2026-09-16, and it is right:** *"I recall there's
a master fader that fades out the track.  It could be that the note is
not playing because the master fader is down.  But I recall that had a
reason to be there.. probably because some plugins would play
continuously if it weren't there."*

Both halves check out.  The fader is `STOP_FADE_SECONDS` in
`shell/clap/src/lib.rs`, and the reason is in its own comment: stop used
to let the instrument ring for the whole ten-second voice grace, and a
patch with a long reverb — `nightdrive`'s `reverb 1.7 0.45` behind a
1.4 s pad release is the case named — stayed audible for most of it,
against `spec/export.md`'s rule that stop is silence.  **The fade is
not the mistake.  Never re-opening it is.**

## The path, exactly

1. `stopped` is `!playing`, recomputed **every block**, not only on the
   falling edge.
2. `stopped_at` is stamped on the falling edge and cleared **only on the
   rising one**.  Nothing else in the file touches it.
3. A key pressed while stopped makes `keyed` true, so the
   `if stopped && !keyed` early return is skipped and the engine
   **does render the note**.
4. Then, unconditionally under `if stopped`, the whole output buffer is
   multiplied by

       g = clamp(1 - (t - stopped_at) / (1.5 * rate), 0, 1) ** 2

5. Past 1.5 s that clamp is `0`, and it stays `0` for as long as the
   transport is stopped.  So the note is rendered and then zeroed.

**The note is not missing; it is silenced after the fact.**  Which is
why nothing about the voice, the allocator or the routing looked wrong:
they are all correct, one stage upstream of a master gain of zero.

## The comment above it already says what it should do

> *Only while stopped, and only over the released tail: a key still held
> is the player's, and nothing here touches a running transport.*

The second clause is true.  **The first is not implemented** — the fade
is applied to the mixed output, which has no idea which voice is a
released tail and which is a hand.

And it contradicts a rule stated two hundred lines above it, in the
falling-edge branch that deliberately releases only the score's voices:
*the piece's notes release now — they live on the timeline — while a
played key, which lives under the player's hands, holds on.*  A key held
**through** a stop is kept by that branch and then faded to nothing by
this one, 1.5 s later.  Two rules in one function, and the fade wins.

## Resolved — 2026-09-16, to his own design

**Henri:** *"The master fader could rise when it receives MIDI signal to
play (and fade back out when there's no MIDI notes playing).  But it's a
minor issue I think."*

That is better than the three options below and it removes the artefact
the first of them was named for.  `stopped_at` is no longer a statement
about the transport; it is a statement about **the tail**.  A hand on
the keys clears it, and letting the last key go re-arms it *from that
instant*, so a ring-out gets its 1.5 s from where it actually began
rather than from a stop three minutes ago.  `key` is `None` the moment a
note is released, so *playing* here means held rather than ringing —
which is the right side of the line, because a ringing tail is precisely
what the fade is for.

**One field was needed and it is the whole subtlety.**  `fade_held`
tells two `None`s apart: *the fade was open and a hand is holding it*
against *there was never a fade*, which is a plugin on a transport that
has not run.  The first draft had no such field, re-armed whenever the
hands were empty, and so started a fade on the first block of any
stopped timeline — fading out an instrument nobody had stopped.  **Two
older tests in `test_export.py` caught it**, by a maximum absolute
difference of 0.011 against the offline render; a played note is
compared sample for sample there, and a fade just beginning is exactly
the kind of error that shape of test is for.  The rising edge clears
the flag too, or a key held across a play would leave it set.

gate: `test/test_export.py::test_a_key_pressed_long_after_the_stop_is_heard`
and `::test_the_stop_fade_closes_again_when_the_hands_are_empty` — the
rise and the fall, each proved by deleting the half it holds.

**And the second of those two was green for the wrong reason first,
which is worth more than the fix.**  Written against `fmpoly` it passed
with the re-arm deleted, because that patch's release is 0.35 s and its
tail dies unaided — a test that could not fail the thing it was named
for.  It is `nightdrive` now, the case the fade's own comment names: a
1.4 s pad release into `reverb 1.7 0.45`, still ringing two seconds
after the key comes up unless something silences it.  With the re-arm
removed it goes red.

## What the fix had to trade — the options weighed before he answered

The fade must stay, for the reason Henri remembered.  What is open is
how a note that starts *after* the stop escapes it.

* **Clear `stopped_at` when a note-on arrives while stopped** — a hand
  on the keys cancels the fade, and the next stop re-arms it.  One line,
  and the whole defect.  Cost: a tail that was mid-fade is restored to
  full gain, which is an audible swell if a key is pressed within 1.5 s
  of the stop.
* **Re-stamp `stopped_at` to now instead** — wrong: the held key would
  then fade out under the player's hand.
* **Fade per voice rather than on the master**, by `started` against
  `stopped_at`.  Correct in every case and much larger: the gain would
  have to move inside the engine, where a voice is still a voice.

*None of the three was taken.*  His own answer above is a fourth and a
better one: the first option's artefact came from cancelling the fade
and never re-arming it, and re-arming on release is what removes it.
