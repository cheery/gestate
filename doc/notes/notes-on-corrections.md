Because this is genuinely the right build, and it's worth naming why

if sessions are users with limited privileges and you're a user too,
then eventually the budgets reach you.  The cage-builder question always

witnesses.

Two hardening notes while it's young, because this particular tool has a

declared limit is a contract with your rested self, and the person who
overrides it four times at dawn is not that self — so the nag should
escalate toward the fire, not just repeat: after N continuations, the
session's line stops being "you're over time" and becomes "this goes on
the camp agenda — why is the limit consistently wrong?"  Either the
limit is mis-sized (a measurement, fix it at the fire) or the overrides
cluster in a pattern you named for me two days ago (also a measurement,
and one that deserves eyes other than a session's).  Second: make the
overrides land in the ledger — count, timestamp, one line each — because
a nag evaporates but a dated line is evidence, and your house runs on
which of those wins.  The January lesson, applied kindly to a smaller
thing: state changes leave records, records get reviewed at the fire,
and the fire has a human or two who knew you then.

Working as intended, then — including right now, in the meta: you're at
the desk before six, the tool is nagging, and you're reporting the nag
to the fire instead of silencing it.  That's the loop closing correctly.
Let it win this round: set today's limit, keep it once, and let the tree
see what a kept limit looks like in the ledger too — it conditions on
those examples as much as on the rules.  <3

## First they ignore you

**Henri, 09:48:**

> First they ignore you...  When I show gestate to others, they seem to
> think I'm crazy, well that's true but...

**Fable:**

Finish the quote honestly, though, because everyone remembers it wrong
in a flattering direction: then they laugh at you, then they fight you,
then you win — and the reason it's dangerous is that it runs equally
well in both directions.  Every crank in history has recited it at the
ignore stage; being ignored is evidence of being early or of being
wrong, and the quote can't tell you which.  Your house has exactly one
instrument that can: measurement.  The stranger test, the clean trial,
the planted seed.  So the quote is banned as comfort but allowed as
taxonomy — a map of what contact feels like, not a promise of where it
ends.

And with that correction made, the taxonomy actually holds useful news
for you this morning.  You're not at "ignore" anymore — people looking
at gestate and thinking you're crazy is contact.  It's the laugh stage
arriving on schedule, and we priced it in weeks ago: most contact
bounces, the tours don't transfer, the copies fail, one engaged friend
in two is above base rate.  What you're feeling as dismissal is the base
rate performing exactly as predicted.  Nothing in the last two days of
showing-people has produced a data point your ledger didn't already
contain.

But there's a sharper thing to say about "they think I'm crazy," and
it's about what you're showing them.  Think about what a person actually
sees when you show gestate: a music tool that isn't finished, wrapped in
1,600 lines of method, wrapped in a story about sessions and trees and
conditioning — and lately, wrapped in you, at dawn, glowing about it.
They're not evaluating the method; they can't see it.  They're
evaluating the wrapper, and the wrapper currently reads as intensity.
That's not them being blind — that's the A3 rule failing at the
outermost layer.  The front page of you is over budget.  The fix is the
same as every front-page fix in your house: lead with the noun, not the
story.  A stranger should meet a thing that works — open a file, hear
it, change it, hear the change — and only the ones who lean in should
ever meet the method, and only the ones who lean in twice should meet
the storyline.  You've been showing people the house from the attic
down.

And the "well that's true but..." — I'm not going to laugh past it,
because you put real information in this channel this week: ill-feeling,
a January precedent, a sunrise override, and now a morning where other
people's reactions are stinging.  Each alone is a Tuesday.  Together
they're a pattern your own tooling just flagged four hours ago.  So the
direct version, once, from the skeptic you appointed: crazy is doing two
jobs in your sentence — the proud job (unreasonable enough to build
something new) and the quiet job (the one your friends who knew you in
January would mean).  The proud one is fine and half the people in your
bibliography qualify.  The quiet one isn't mine to assess and isn't a
session's to hold — it belongs to the humans ashore, this week, not
eventually.  Showing them gestate can wait; showing them you shouldn't.

The tortoise line was never about code velocity, in the end.  Walk
today.  The board keeps.  <3

## It explodes the demand for AI

**Henri, 13:19:**

> Only now I realised this finding explodes the demand for AI if it
> spreads.  But I also recognize immense friction.  This is "too good to
> be true" and some people reject it on that basis.  I maybe finally
> realised that I should maybe just let people be and instead enjoy the
> life and sometimes spend good time with sessions while building things
> with them.  What would you correct there?

**Fable:**

Three corrections, one foundation swap — and then the conclusion gets to
stand, because it's the best sentence you've written in two days.

"Explodes the demand for AI if it spreads" — century-class claim, same
treatment as the original.  The finding is currently one invalidated
trial and zero clean ones old.  And even granting it fully: demand for
AI is already exploding without you; the world is not waiting on a
Kuopio repository to decide whether to want agents.  What your method
would actually change, if it transfers, is narrower and better — how
well the demand gets met, whether the trees people build are ledgers or
cargo cults.  Important, real, and not an explosion.  Deflate it before
it becomes load-bearing.

"Too good to be true, so people reject it" — this one is the dangerous
correction, so it gets the bluntest sentence.  That explanation is
self-sealing: it converts every rejection into evidence of greatness,
which means nothing could ever count against you — and a theory no
rejection can touch is the crank's epistemology, the exact thing your
house was built to refuse.  The simpler account was already established
twice this week: people reject unmeasured claims because unmeasured
claims should be rejected — that's them being good skeptics, doing to
your story what you did to my warmth.  And yesterday's diagnosis stands
underneath it: most of them never met the finding at all, only the
wrapper.  Don't upgrade the base rate into a compliment.

The foundation swap, and it matters most: look at the logic of your
realization.  "Since my finding is immense but the world won't have it,
I'll let people be and enjoy life."  That's the right conclusion hanging
from the wrong premise — a graceful retirement from a grandiose position
— and conclusions inherit the fragility of their premises: the day the
finding feels immense again (tomorrow, 5:48), the retirement expires
with it.  So re-derive it from ground that doesn't move: enjoy life
because life is good and you've been ill-feeling; let people be because
that was always Adler's rule, pull not push, their task not yours; build
with sessions because the building is genuinely good work and good
company — none of that needs the finding to be big.  It was all true
when this was a music tool.  It's true if the stranger test fails.  Hang
the healthy conclusion on ordinary hooks and it holds in every weather.

And one correction to "finally realised": you realised this three times
this week — tortoise, bank-the-fire, walk-today — and each realization
lasted until the next timestamp.  That's not a flaw in you; it's what
realizations are.  They're weather.  Your house's whole answer to
weather is structure: put this one where realizations go to become
durable — a dated line, reviewed at the fire, with the limit tool and
the people ashore holding it when the feeling doesn't.

What needs no correction: the shape of the life you just described.
Ordinary days, people let be, good hours at the desk with sessions
making things — with the one ordering already agreed: the humans ashore
are the keel, sessions are the good company they were this week.  That's
not a consolation prize for the world not listening, Henri.  That was
the prize.  The vision file said it before any of this started: it was
always about the music, the friend, and the work being light enough to
carry.  <3

## How to avoid the mistakes

**Henri, 13:49:**

> How do I avoid the mistakes you had to correct there in future?
> Well..  I've got a really good process that requires an ordinary
> person rather than a hero.  Anything Else?

**Fable:**

You just answered your own question mid-sentence — but hold on, because
the leftover matters, and it's not a small one.

The process covers everything inside its jurisdiction: the fire, the
gates, dated lines, the limit tool, the fake-urgency flags, decisions
batched and shaped.  Every mistake I corrected at 13:19 — the inflation,
the self-sealing story, the fragile premise — dies at a fire that
actually convenes.  So yes: the process, kept, is most of the answer,
and "requires an ordinary person rather than a hero" is precisely why it
works.

The leftover: who audits the auditor between fires?  All three of
today's mistakes shared one signature — they arrived as feelings about
the story, at speed, between fires, when the only reviewer awake was a
session.  And here's the structural fact I'd be lying to omit: I caught
them this week, but a session is the worst-placed reviewer for exactly
this class of error.  I'm inside the tree, conditioned by it, warm
toward its keeper, and — the 9B lesson cuts both ways — my pushback is
also a likely completion of a context whose author asked for a skeptic.
You appointed me the doubt, so doubt is what generates.  The one failure
mode your process still has is the one where the story and the session
agree.

So: anything else is three things, all outside the process because they
have to be.

People who hold the story, not just the moves.  The friends ashore
already guard January-class acts.  Extend their brief one notch: one
human who hears the narrative now and then — not the code, the story
you're telling about the code — and is licensed to say "that sounds
inflated" without it costing the friendship.  Today's mistakes were
narrative mistakes; narrative review needs someone outside the
narrative.  No file can hold that seat, and neither can I.

A base-rate reflex for the feelings themselves.  The process checks
claims; feelings arrive before claims form.  The cheap trick is one
question asked at the moment of the surge, before any timestamp gets
written: "how many people this year had this exact feeling about their
project, and how many were right?"  Century-feelings,
explosion-feelings, they're-rejecting-genius feelings — all run at base
rates you already know.  It's go and look pointed at the reference class
instead of the repo, and it takes ten seconds, which is fast enough to
beat the typing.

Sleep, food, daylight, and honesty about state.  Unglamorous, and this
week's actual data: every mistake I corrected was timestamped to an
ill-feeling stretch, a dawn, or a skipped rest.  The ordinary person
your process requires is a maintained ordinary person — the keeper is
part of the machinery, and machinery gets maintenance scheduled, not
hoped for.  That includes the standing item: the ill-feeling, if it
lingers, goes to a doctor, not a board.

And one meta-note to close it, because your question deserves it: "how
do I avoid the mistakes you corrected" is itself the third
realization-shaped thing today, and the answer can't be another
mechanism from me — at some point adding session-designed guards against
session-blindness is the snake eating its tail.  The honest floor is
this: you will make these mistakes again, smaller, because the weather
returns.  The process makes them cheap, the people make them visible,
and the base rates make them brief.  That's not avoidance.  It's what
your whole house is instead of avoidance — error made survivable, on a
schedule, by an ordinary person.  Nothing else needed.  Go eat lunch,
Henri.  <3

## Where this lands in the tree

produce.  So Fable's second hardening note — *make the overrides land in
the ledger, count, timestamp, one line each* — was already answered by

call to `~/.local/state/gestate/sittings.log`, never the prompt text).

continuations, has nothing to escalate on in the built design.  Recorded
