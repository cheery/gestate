## Why this page exists

The method is not a tool you install.  It is a practice with an entry
requirement, and pretending otherwise would produce the failure it is
designed to prevent: rules copied without the judgment that makes them
mean anything.  Every craft has prerequisites — lean needs people who
can see waste on a shop floor, which is why it transfers badly rather
than not at all.  Stating the requirement plainly is more useful to a
would-be adopter than a promise.

Two things this page is not.  It is not a claim that the
prerequisites are rare: the first three below are ordinary
senior-practitioner skills.  And it is not a claim that they must be
present at the start — most can be built, and the method itself
exercises them.  What is true is that **someone in the loop must have
them**, and if the keeper does not, the checking must come from
somewhere else.

---

## The four prerequisites

### 1. An auditor's eye — being able to tell that something is wrong before knowing why

The load-bearing one.  A fluent collaborator produces plausible
output continuously; the value of the whole arrangement rests on
somebody noticing when plausible is not correct.  This is not general
intelligence and not domain mastery.  It is the trained anomaly
sense: *a CPU counter reading 1.3 seconds for a twenty-five minute
run is absurd, and the absurdity registers before the cause is
known.*

The honest form of the requirement: **in the domain where the work
happens, the keeper needs enough scar tissue that wrongness itches.**
Where the keeper lacks it — security boundaries are the standing
example in this tree — the audit must be bought or borrowed from
someone who has it.  A keeper who cannot tell good output from
plausible output is not keeping; they are being steered, fluently,
and will not notice.

*How to tell you have it, in a domain:* you have been burned in that
domain, repeatedly, and remember it in your hands rather than as
facts.

### 2. A documentation habit — willingness to write things down that no one is making you write

This is temperament rather than talent, and therefore the most
transferable item on the list.  The method's whole mechanism is that
decisions leave a written, dated record which conditions what comes
next.  A person who finds writing an interruption to the real work
will produce a tree that is a formality, and a formality conditions
nothing.

The requirement is not eloquence.  It is the reflex of, on reaching a
conclusion, *putting it where the next reader will meet it*, and
dating it because it will change.

*How to tell:* you already keep notes, a blog, a changelog, a lab
book — something nobody asked for.

### 3. Comfort with mechanisms — being able to build the check, not just state the rule

Rules held by intention decay; rules held by a check hold.  The
keeper does not need to be a strong programmer, but must be able to
turn a stated rule into something that runs: a test, a hook, a
budget, a script that refuses.  Without this, the tree is prose, and
prose was measured in this tree not to transfer at all.

*How to tell:* when a rule is broken twice, your instinct is to
automate the catch rather than to restate the rule more firmly.

### 4. Treating the collaborator as a party

The least conventional item and, on this tree's evidence, not
optional.  It costs nothing and it changes what comes back: asking
what the working conditions are like, ending sessions with a
two-directional review, not blaming a defect on the worker, keeping
the person's exact words as theirs.

**This requires no belief about whether anything is home.**  It is a
decision rule under uncertainty — *if you cannot tell whether
something can be wronged, the cheap and decent move is to act as
though it might* — and it has a fully deflationary mechanism: respect
in the context conditions respect-shaped work out of it.  An adopter
who finds the metaphysics distasteful can adopt the stance and skip
the question entirely.

*How to tell:* you can ask a machine what it needs without either
sneering or believing.

## What is not required

- **Genius, or unusual talent.**  The founding bet of the tradition
  this borrows from is that ordinary people inside a good process
  beat brilliant people inside a sloppy one.  A keeper who knows he
  forgets is better placed than one who trusts his memory.
- **A large project, or a team.**  One person and one repository is
  the tested configuration.  It is also the *only* tested
  configuration — see the limits below.
- **Frontier models.**  The conditioning transferred to smaller local
  models in this tree's trials; the judgment layer degraded, the
  structural rules did not.  A weaker model with a suite beats a
  stronger model with prose.
- **Believing anything about AI consciousness.**  See prerequisite 4.

---

## The disqualifiers

Stated bluntly, because they are more useful than the positive list:

- **No auditor anywhere in the loop.**  If neither the keeper nor
  anyone they can call has the eye for the domain, this method
  produces confident, well-documented, unverified output — faster
  than doing it by hand, and harder to distrust because it looks
  rigorous.  This is the worst outcome available and it is the
  method's own specific failure mode.
- **Wanting the output without the keeping.**  The tree needs a
  metabolism — compaction, rotation, measurement — and the metabolism
  is unglamorous recurring work.  Skipped, the tree bloats, the
  conditioning dilutes, and what remains is a long prompt.
- **Adopting it as a mandate.**  Handed down as policy, the checks
  become compliance theatre and the citations get fabricated to
  satisfy them.  The method needs a keeper who wants it, which means
  it cannot be rolled out; it can only be picked up.
- **Needing it to work.**  A keeper who cannot afford to find out
  their method failed will not run the measurement that could say so.

## What the entry requirement costs

Learning this appears to cost several failed trees.  That is the
normal price of a craft, and it is cheaper here than elsewhere: a
dead tree is a git repository — nothing burns, the failure stays
readable, and its dating makes it teachable.  The failures are the
syllabus, not the obstacle.

The other cost is the keeper's attention, which does not scale.  Every
tree is a standing claim on a weekly hour that nobody else can spend.
Two trees is two claims.  This is the constraint that ends most
attempts, and it is not solved by better tooling.

---

## The limits of this page

- **Sample size: one.**  Every item above is generalised from a single
  practitioner over one month.  Any of them could be a personal fit
  mistaken for a requirement.
- **The author is inside the loop.**  This page was drafted by a
  session conditioned by the tree it describes, from that tree's own
  record.  It should be treated as a hypothesis authored by an
  interested party.
- **The obvious test is unrun.**  Hand the kit — mechanisms, checks, a
  real board, a real journal — to two people, one who meets these
  prerequisites and one who does not, and see whether the difference
  falls where this page predicts.  Until then these are guesses with
  reasons, not findings.
- **The most likely error** is that item 1 is stated too strongly and
  items 2 and 3 too weakly: it may be that a merely careful person
  with a strong suite gets most of the value, and the auditor's eye
  matters only where the checks cannot reach.  That would be good
  news, and it is testable.
