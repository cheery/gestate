## 1. The keeper's first week

Standard work for a tree you are about to own.

Not a framework.  Not advice.  Not a demand.

**Speak:** This is one project's working arrangement, shown to you
because you asked twice.  Nothing in these slides asks you to believe
anything — every claim has a date and a file in the tree behind me, and
you can check any of them before deciding.  What you leave with is a
week's work, small enough to see all of.

## 2. The premise

Things told to a session are **lost** — unless they are
written down.

You remember little between months.

So: everything lives in the tree — and anything in the
tree that is wrong **will be believed**.

**Speak:** This is the whole design.  Whatever you tell a session is
gone when the conversation ends; only what reaches the tree survives to
the next one.  I rediscovered within weeks that I have the same problem
on a month's delay, and so does any stranger reading cold.  One fix
serves all three readers.  Demonstrate, don't assert: open the tree,
show a rule, show the test that holds it, break it, show the commit
refused.

## 3. The safety rule

**Never open an unknown tree, or an unknown LLM session,
on an unsafe machine.**

**Speak:** The premise cuts both ways.  A tree conditions whatever
reads it — that is the point — so a tree written by someone else
conditions your session just as well, including maliciously, and a
session with tools is an actor, not a viewer: opening one inside an
unknown tree executes that tree's intent on your machine.  The rule you
already have for running unknown code applies unchanged to the two new
things.  A strange tree is read as text, on a safe machine or in a
sandbox, before any session is opened in it; and an unknown session
gets a fence — mine denies its sessions' own sudo on purpose, and I can
show the fence saying so.  This practice is being reinvented elsewhere
without this rule, and that is the unsafe version of it.

## 4. Day zero — the first tasks

Before any ritual, three documents:

* **vision** — what this is for
* **manifesto** — how the work is done
* **author** — what you spend attention on, and what each party owes

Then: **let a session reflect on them, and discuss it with you.**

**Speak:** These are my tree's names — `vision.md`, `manifesto.md`,
`spec/author.md` — and yours may differ; the three questions they
answer may not.  Select or write first versions: short, dated, and a
line earns its place by having decided something.  Then the first act
of the collaboration is not a task — it is handing the three to a
session and talking them through.  What the session reflects back shows
you the gap between what the documents say and what you meant, while
the gap is still cheap.  And it is the honest start of the contract:
the session learns what it is inside of, you learn what you actually
wrote.

## 5. The doubt

*"I am really uncertain that I am up for this task."*

— the author of the tree this comes from, 2026-08.

The doubt is the qualification.

**Speak:** That sentence is mine and it is in the public tree with a
date.  This method exists because its author distrusts his memory, his
unmeasured claims and his own enthusiasm; the distrust, wired into
files and a suite, is why it works.  If you feel that doubt, you are
holding the qualification, not the disqualification — the next slide
is who actually fails.

## 6. Known failures

* the **cargo-culter** — copies the sentences, not the suite
* the **metric abuser** — feeds the number instead of the work
* the **mirror-shrine keeper** — hears the tree echo him back,
  and calls it confirmation

What they share is **confidence**.

**Speak:** Every failure mode this method predicts for itself begins
with somebody who was sure.  Each of the three has a guard in my tree,
built after a near-miss, with a date: copy-the-mechanisms-not-the-prose
was measured, not preached — the prose-only copy failed its trial;
a number nobody asked for is a number nobody checks, so no lamp is
built ahead of its defect; and a session's praise of the method is the
method grading itself, so the real checks are routed to strangers.
Show one guard for real.

## 7. Fix the task, not the person

When the job feels too big —

make the **task** smaller.  Never the person bigger.

**Speak:** Held in the head, where doubt inflates it, keeping a tree is
a fog.  Written on a page it is five acts, thirty to sixty minutes.
Toyota never asked for heroic operators; it wrote standard work so the
job could be done on a tired Tuesday.  This deck is the task, fixed —
and when the doubt turns up mid-week, the answer is not introspection,
it is *go and look*: your done-pile and journal will record the
allegedly too-hard parts already being done, with dates.  In this
house it is written down which of those wins.

## 8. The job, visibly small

| act | when | about |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Read the lamps | every fire | 1 min |
| 2. Open the decisions batch | every fire | 10–20 min |
| 3. Measure one rule | every fire | 10 min |
| 4. One pass over the pile | every fire | 10 min |
| 5. Rotate the journal | when a lamp says — monthly | 30 min |

**Start with only one.  Perfect it before adding the next.**

**Speak:** Five acts, thirty to sixty minutes, weekly as a shape, not a
law.  But you do not start with five — you start with one, and my
advice is act 1 the moment you own a lamp, because it is one minute and
it teaches the posture: read the instrument, not your memory.  Perfect
one act — it happened, it was on the page, it produced its finding —
before the next exists.  The cadence is yours to move; what is not
yours to move is doing them in the head instead of on the page.

## 9. Three habits under everything

A claim with no file, test or number is **unfinished**.

A defect is a **caller** — the deliverable is the mechanism.

**Go and look** — before explaining, describe what it did.

**Speak:** These are the ground the five acts stand on, and they are
most of the method.  "It is fast" is not a statement; a millisecond
count with the command that produced it is.  When something breaks, a
fix plus an apology is half the work — the other half is the check that
makes that class of mistake fail loudly next time, usually a test under
a second.  And most confident explanations in my tree turned out wrong;
there is a register of them, dated.  Show the register.

## 10. Act 1 — read the lamps.  You have none yet.

One command, green or lit.  A lit lamp is not a failure —
it wants an act, not a panic, and it will still be lit next week.

Day one rule: **build the instrument the moment the need arises.**

**Speak:** In my tree this act is one command, twelve checks, fourteen
seconds, and a page with two rows.  You start with zero lamps and you
do not build twelve — you build one: the cheapest check that would have
caught your *last* mistake.  That is how every lamp here was born; each
one cites the defect that paid for it, and I can show you the ledger.
A number nobody asked for is a number nobody checks — so never build a
lamp ahead of its defect.

## 11. Act 2 — decisions arrive shaped, or not at all

A question that reaches the keeper carries:

* the question in **one line**
* two or three **named options, with costs**
* a **default with a trigger** — *"if undecided by Friday, X"*
* **reversibility stated**
* the recommendation marked *suspected*

Approving a default is ~10× cheaper than composing an answer.

**Speak:** A blank-page question is why decisions pile up.  Most
questions die before reaching you: half are already answered by the
tree, and reversible-plus-measurable ones are experiments, not
decisions — try, measure, keep or revert.  What survives arrives
shaped, and **batched**: five in one sitting cost less than five across
a week.  A blank question reaching you is a defect in whoever sent it —
hold the sender to the shape, not yourself to the blank page.

## 12. Act 3 — measure one rule against the week that happened

One rule.  Not an audit.

Closed book first: write what it says from memory, *then* open it.

Three outcomes: **it held** · **broken, nothing noticed** ·
**nobody could have followed it**

Two of the three are findings.

**Speak:** Broken-and-nothing-noticed means the rule is prose, and the
deliverable is a mechanism, not a resolution to be more careful.
Nobody-could-have-followed-it means the rule is fat or wrong, and
either way your rule budget gets a line back.  The closed-book step is
a reading on both your recall and the rule — one you cannot approximate
from memory is one nobody could have followed, arriving early.  Picking
one rule is what makes this finishable; an audit is what makes it
skipped.

## 13. Act 4 — one pass over the shelf

One question per shelved card, and it is the only question:

**Is this waiting on an event, or on me?**

Event → sediment.  Costs nothing.  Leave it.

You → debt.  It is not shelved, it is blocked on a decision.
Move it into act 2.

**Speak:** A deep shelf is a rich board, not a fat one — but only half
the time, and this pass is which half.  The trap is tidying a debt card
by re-shelving it with a better sentence.  Show two real cards from my
shelf, one of each kind, and let them sort a third.

## 14. Act 5 — rotate the journal

Skim the closing month once.  At heading level.

Promote only what a stranger needs to **follow** a rule —
what they'd only need to **believe** it stays journal.

**Most months promote nothing, and that is the expected result.**

One index line.  Close the file.  Never rewrite the past.

**Speak:** Git already remembers; a retroactively edited journal is a
second source of truth about the past, and my rules file names the
refusal: archive, don't airbrush.  My tree keeps its wrong entries
corrected in place, with the correction dated — it teaches partly by
visibly having been wrong, and so will yours.

## 15. Kaizen — both parties learn from their mistakes

Four questions, at the end of a sitting:

**what did we learn · what did we get wrong ·
what mechanism does that earn · what is tomorrow's first thing**

The session asks **you** first — and waits.

A kaizen with one participant is a session grading itself.

**Speak:** The session's mistakes are cheap to catch — a measurement
finds them.  Yours are not: no suite can notice a choice made too fast,
so this dialogue is the only instrument pointed at your half.  Two
rules keep it honest, both paid for in my tree with a date: the session
asks and *waits*, because answers delivered first become the frame; and
the entry is written only when both halves are in it.  The third
question is the one that turns talk into a tree — a lesson that earns
no mechanism is a resolution, and resolutions do not survive a month.

## 16. What this must never become

A demand.

No streak.  No red gate for a missed evening.
Skipping three weeks and doing one act on the fourth
is the ritual **working**.

**Speak:** The tree that carries your values has to survive your
absences, including the ordinary ones, or it is not safe to own.  Build
no reminder that fires unprompted; lamps speak when looked at, where
you already stand.  And a warning from my own tree, measured, not
theoretical: the tree cannot supply conviction.  What your project is
for, which door, when to stop — that five per cent is structurally
yours, and a tree that tells you otherwise is a mirror.  Keep anchors
that are not the tree.

## 17. Everything you just saw is prose

Prose conditions by hope.  A mechanism conditions by fact.

First question of any rule: **what fails when it is broken?**

Your deliverable this week is not notes on this talk.
It is day zero, and your first gate.

**Speak:** These slides are the weakest form the method takes, and we
measured that: a derived document of the method, handed alone to a
reader with no tree, conditioned nobody — 2026-08-21, it is in the
trial directory, kept as evidence.  What transfers is the mechanism,
never the sentences.  So the homework: the three documents of day zero
and the session dialogue over them; a board where every card states a
problem and its *because*; one gate that refuses your last mistake; a
journal file the rotation will one day close.  And one act of the five,
done once, on the page — perfected before the next.  Next week we read
your lamp — the one.
